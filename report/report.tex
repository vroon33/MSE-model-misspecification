\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The problem}

When we fit a model to data, we pick a loss function and choose the parameters that make the
loss as small as possible. The two most common losses are the mean squared error (MSE) and the mean absolute error (MAE). The survey by Terven et al.~\cite{terven} gives rules for choosing between them, for example that MAE is better when the data has outliers. But it doesn't explain these rules using estimation theory.

Minimizing a loss is the same as maximising likelihood. So every loss assumes some
distribution for the noise. We later show in Section~\ref{sec:losses} we show that MSE assumes Gaussian noise and MAE assumes Laplace noise.

In this project we observe what happens when this assumption is wrong. For example, if we use MSE but the noise is actually Laplace, does the estimate still be correct on average? what about the variance? Intuitively we'd expect variance to be larger than that of the best possible estimator.

We measure this loss in accuracy using the Cram\'er--Rao lower bound (CRLB), which provides a lower bound for the variance of any unbiased estimator ~\cite{kay}. For our study, we find the ratio of the CRLB for the true noise, to the variance of our estimator,
\[
  \text{efficiency ratio} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)}{\text{CRLB}}.
\]
A ratio of 1 simply means the estimator is as good as possible. A ratio of 2 means its variance is twice the CRLB, hence it needs twice as many samples to be as accurate as the best estimator.

\subsection{The setup}

We start with the simplest case, which is estimating a DC level in noise, as in Kay. We observe $N$ samples
\begin{equation}
  x[n] = A + w[n], \qquad n = 0, 1, \dots, N-1,
  \label{eq:dc}
\end{equation}
where $A \in \mathbb{R}$ is the unknown constant and $w[n]$ is the noise. The noise samples $w[n]$ are i.i.d.\ with zero mean and an assumed density $p_w(w)$ that is symmetric about zero, so $p_w(-w) = p_w(w)$. We want to estimate the parameter $A$ from the samples.

Stacking the samples and the noise into vectors,
\[
  \mathbf{x} = [x[0], x[1], \dots, x[N-1]]^\top, \qquad
  \mathbf{w} = [w[0], w[1], \dots, w[N-1]]^\top,
\]
the model becomes
\[
  \mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{w},
\]
where $\mathbf{1} = [1, 1, \dots, 1]^\top$ is the column vector of $N$ ones. This is a special case of linear regression, $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta} + \mathbf{w}$, with $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta} = A$. Here $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{1} = N$, so the $1/N$ in our results becomes
$(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ for the general regression model, which we study later.

\subsection{Outline}

Section~\ref{sec:losses} shows that squared error and absolute error are the maximum likelihood
losses for Gaussian and Laplace noise. Sections~\ref{sec:crlb} and~\ref{sec:mismatch} compute the
CRLB for these two noises and the variance of the mean and the median under each, and
Section~\ref{sec:sims} checks the results by simulation, including skewed noise. The next two
sections extend this to two families of noise. Section~\ref{sec:studentt} makes the tails heavier
with the Student-t family, and Section~\ref{sec:gamma} makes the noise skewed with the gamma family.
Section~\ref{sec:mcrb} then introduces the misspecified Cram\'er--Rao bound, which is the tight bound
for an estimator built on a wrong noise model and explains all the earlier results in one formula.
Section~\ref{sec:pareto} looks at Pareto noise, where the regularity conditions behind these bounds
fail, and Section~\ref{sec:realdata} applies the same ideas to real insurance claim data.

\section{Losses as noise assumptions}
\label{sec:losses}

In this section we derive the MSE and MAE losses. In both cases we write the likelihood of the samples for an assumed noise distribution and find the $A$ that maximises it.

From \eqref{eq:dc}, $w[n] = x[n] - A$, so each sample $x[n]$ has density $p_w(x[n]-A)$. The
samples are i.i.d., so the likelihood is the product of these densities,
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf{x}; A) = \prod_{n=0}^{N-1} p_w(x[n]-A).
  \label{eq:lik}
\end{equation}
The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is the value of $A$ that makes this as large as possible
for the observed samples. It is easier to work with the log of the likelihood. Since the log is an increasing function, the maximum is at the same $A$, so
\[
  \hat A = \arg\max_A \, p(\mathbf{x}; A) = \arg\max_A \, \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)
  = \arg\max_A \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \ln p_w(x[n]-A).
\]

\subsection{Gaussian noise gives squared error}

Let the noise be Gaussian with zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$,
\[
  w[n] \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), \qquad
  p_w(w) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{w^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
\]
Then $x[n] \sim \mathcal{N}(A, \sigma^2)$, and from \eqref{eq:lik} the likelihood is
\begin{align*}
  p(\mathbf{x}; A) &= \prod_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x[n]-A)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \\
  &= \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x[n]-A)^2\right).
\end{align*}
Taking the log gives
\[
  \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A) = -\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)
  - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x[n]-A)^2 .
\]
The first term does not depend on $A$. The second term has a minus sign, so maximising
$\ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)$ is the same as minimising
\[
  J(A) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x[n]-A)^2 .
\]
This is the squared error loss. So minimising MSE is the same as finding the MLE under Gaussian
noise.

Setting the derivative of $J$ to zero,
\begin{align*}
  \frac{dJ}{dA} = -2\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x[n]-A) &= 0 \\
  \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] - NA &= 0 .
\end{align*}
We thus obtain
\[
  \boxed{\hat A = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] = \bar{x}}
\]
So under Gaussian noise, the MLE of $A$ is the sample mean.

\subsection{Laplace noise gives absolute error}

Now let the noise be Laplace with zero mean and a scale parameter $b > 0$,
\[
  w[n] \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \operatorname{Laplace}(0, b), \qquad
  p_w(w) = \frac{1}{2b}\exp\!\left(-\frac{|w|}{b}\right).
\]
This density is also symmetric about zero, but its tails are heavier because the Gaussian density falls off like $e^{-w^2}$, while the Laplace density only falls off like $e^{-|w|}$. So large noise
values are more likely with Laplace noise, thus the data might tend to have a few samples far away from the rest.

We follow the same steps as in the Gaussian case. From \eqref{eq:lik} the likelihood is
\begin{align*}
  p(\mathbf{x}; A) &= \prod_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{1}{2b}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{|x[n]-A|}{b}\right) \\
  &= \frac{1}{(2b)^N}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{b}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|x[n]-A|\right),
\end{align*}
and its log is
\[
  \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A) = -N\ln(2b) - \frac{1}{b}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|x[n]-A| .
\]
Again the first term does not depend on $A$, so maximising $\ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)$ is the same as
minimising
\[
  J(A) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|x[n]-A| .
\]
This is the absolute error loss. So minimising MAE is the same as finding the MLE under Laplace noise.

Here we cannot just set the derivative to zero, because $|x[n]-A|$ has a sharp edge at $A = x[n]$ where its non-differentiable. Everywhere else the derivative is
\[
  \frac{dJ}{dA} = -\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{sign}(x[n]-A),
\]
Hence, each sample above $A$ adds $-1$ and each sample below $A$ adds $+1$. So the derivative is the number of samples below
$A$ minus the number of samples above $A$.

If more samples lie above $A$ than below it, the derivative is negative and $J$ decreases as $A$
increases. Once more samples lie below $A$, the derivative is positive and $J$ increases. As $A$
moves from left to right it passes the samples one by one, so the derivative goes up in steps,
from $-N$ to $+N$. So $J$ is smallest where the derivative changes sign, which is where half the
samples lie on each side of $A$. For odd $N$ the derivative never equals zero. It jumps from $-1$
to $+1$ at the middle sample, so the minimum is at this corner of $J$, which is the sample median,
\[
  \boxed{\hat A = \operatorname{median}\bigl(x[0], x[1], \dots, x[N-1]\bigr)}
\]
For example, with the samples $1$, $2$ and $10$, the derivative is $-3, -1, +1, +3$ as $A$ passes
each sample, so $J$ is smallest at $A = 2$. Thus, under Laplace noise, the MLE of $A$ is the sample median. 

To summarise, MSE assumes Gaussian noise and gives the mean, and MAE assumes Laplace noise and gives the median!

\section{The Cram\'er--Rao lower bound}
\label{sec:crlb}

The CRLB gives a lower bound on the variance of any unbiased estimator of $A$. From
Kay~\cite[Theorem 3.1]{kay}, if the likelihood satisfies the regularity condition
\[
  E\!\left[\frac{\partial \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)}{\partial A}\right] = 0 \quad \text{for all } A,
\]
then every unbiased estimator $\hat A$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(\hat A) \geq \frac{1}{I(A)}, \qquad
  I(A) = E\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)}{\partial A}\right)^{2}\right],
  \label{eq:crlb}
\end{equation}
where $I(A)$ is the Fisher information. When $\ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)$ is twice differentiable, the
Fisher information can also be written as
\begin{equation}
  I(A) = -E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)}{\partial A^2}\right].
  \label{eq:fisher2}
\end{equation}

From \eqref{eq:lik}, the log-likelihood is a sum over the samples, so its derivative is also a
sum,
\[
  \frac{\partial \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)}{\partial A} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} s(x[n]; A), \qquad
  s(x; A) = \frac{\partial}{\partial A} \ln p_w(x - A).
\]
Each term $s(x[n]; A)$ depends on only one sample, so the terms are independent, and each has
zero mean by the regularity condition. The variance of a sum of independent zero-mean terms is
the sum of their variances, so
\begin{equation}
  I(A) = E\!\left[\Bigl(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} s(x[n]; A)\Bigr)^{2}\right]
  = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} E\!\left[s(x[n]; A)^2\right] = N\, i(A), \qquad
  i(A) = E\!\left[s(x; A)^2\right].
  \label{eq:fisher-iid}
\end{equation}
So we only need $i(A)$, the Fisher information of a single sample.

\subsection{CRLB for Gaussian noise}

For Gaussian noise,
\[
  \ln p_w(x - A) = -\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(x-A)^2}{2\sigma^2}.
\]
Differentiating with respect to $A$,
\[
  s(x; A) = \frac{x - A}{\sigma^2} = \frac{w}{\sigma^2}.
\]
So the information in one sample is
\[
  i(A) = \frac{E[w^2]}{\sigma^4} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^4} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}.
\]
The second form also \eqref{eq:fisher2} gives the same answer, since
$\partial^2 \ln p_w(x-A)/\partial A^2 = -1/\sigma^2$ for every $x$. Thus using the i.i.d additivity property of Fisher information,
$I(A) = N/\sigma^2$, and we obtain
\[
  \boxed{\text{CRLB}_{\text{Gauss}} = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}}
\]

\subsection{CRLB for Laplace noise}

For Laplace noise,
\[
  \ln p_w(x - A) = -\ln(2b) - \frac{|x - A|}{b}.
\]
For $x \neq A$, the derivative with respect to $A$ is
\[
  s(x; A) = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(x - A)}{b} = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(w)}{b}.
\]
The sign is either $+1$ or $-1$, so $s(x; A)^2 = 1/b^2$ for every sample. Then
\[
  i(A) = E\!\left[\frac{1}{b^2}\right] = \frac{1}{b^2},
\]
so $I(A) = N/b^2$, and we thus obtain
\[
  \boxed{\text{CRLB}_{\text{Laplace}} = \frac{b^2}{N}}
\]

The regularity condition holds, because the Laplace density is symmetric and so
\[
  E[s(x; A)] = \frac{1}{b}\bigl(P(w > 0) - P(w < 0)\bigr) = \frac{1}{b}\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\right) = 0.
\]
The derivative does not exist at $x = A$, but this single point has probability zero, so it does
not change any of the expectations.

Note that we could only use the first form \eqref{eq:crlb} of Fisher information here. The second derivative of $|x - A|$ is zero everywhere except at $x = A$, so the second form
\eqref{eq:fisher2} would give $i(A) = 0$ and an infinite bound, which is wrong! As mentioned above, the second form needs $\ln p_w$ to be twice differentiable, and it's not the case here with the Laplacian noise!
\newline
Kay~\cite{kay} also states that an unbiased estimator reaches the CRLB only if we can write an expression of the type:
\[
  \frac{\partial \ln p(\mathbf{x}; A)}{\partial A} = I(A)\bigl(g(\mathbf{x}) - A\bigr)
\]
for some function $g$. Under Gaussian noise this holds with $g(\mathbf{x}) = \bar x$. Under Laplace noise the derivative is $\frac{1}{b}\sum_n \operatorname{sign}(x[n] - A)$, which is a
step function of $A$ and cannot be written in this form. So for a fixed $N$, no unbiased estimator reaches the Laplace
bound exactly. In Section~\ref{sec:median} we see that the median reaches it as $N$ grows!

\section{Estimators under the wrong noise}
\label{sec:mismatch}

We now find the variance of the sample mean and the sample median under both noise types, and
compare each with the CRLB from Section~\ref{sec:crlb}.

\subsection{The sample mean}

The sample mean is unbiased for any noise with zero mean,
\[
  E[\bar x] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} E\bigl[A + w[n]\bigr] = A.
\]
The samples are independent, so
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{Var}(w[n])
  = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(w)}{N}.
  \label{eq:varmean}
\end{equation}
This only uses the variance of the noise so far, without considering the shape of a distribution.
\subsubsection{Under Gaussian Noise}
Under Gaussian noise, $\operatorname{Var}(w) = \sigma^2$, so $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) =
\sigma^2/N$. This equals the CRLB, so the sample mean is efficient under Gaussian noise.

\subsubsection{Under Laplacian Noise}

Under Laplace noise, the noise has zero mean, so $\operatorname{Var}(w) = E[w^2] - (E[w])^2
= E[w^2]$. Writing out the expectation,
\[
  E[w^2] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} w^2 \, \frac{1}{2b} e^{-|w|/b} \, dw .
\]
\[
  E[w^2] = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2b}\int_{0}^{\infty} w^2 e^{-w/b} \, dw
  = \frac{1}{b}\int_{0}^{\infty} w^2 e^{-w/b} \, dw .
\]
We find the integral using integration by parts twice. Let
\[
  I_k = \int_{0}^{\infty} w^k e^{-w/b} \, dw .
\]
Using integration by parts: 
\[
  \int_0^\infty u \, dv = \Bigl[uv\Bigr]_0^\infty - \int_0^\infty v \, du .
\]
For $I_2$ we take $u = w^2$ and $dv = e^{-w/b}\,dw$, so $du = 2w\,dw$ and $v = -b\,e^{-w/b}$.
Then
\begin{align*}
  I_2 &= \Bigl[-b\,w^2 e^{-w/b}\Bigr]_0^\infty + 2b\int_0^\infty w\, e^{-w/b}\,dw \\
      &= 0 + 2b\, I_1 .
\end{align*}
The boundary term is zero, because at $w = 0$ the factor $w^2$ is zero, and as $w \to \infty$ the exponential $e^{-w/b}$ goes to zero faster than $w^2$ grows. In the same way, for $I_1$ we
take $u = w$ and $dv = e^{-w/b}\,dw$,
\begin{align*}
  I_1 &= \Bigl[-b\,w\, e^{-w/b}\Bigr]_0^\infty + b\int_0^\infty e^{-w/b}\,dw \\
      &= 0 + b\Bigl[-b\, e^{-w/b}\Bigr]_0^\infty \\
      &= b \cdot b = b^2 .
\end{align*}
Putting these together, $I_2 = 2b \cdot b^2 = 2b^3$, and so
\begin{align*}
  \operatorname{Var}(w) = E[w^2] &= \frac{1}{b}\, I_2 \\
  &= 2b^2 .
\end{align*}
Now from \eqref{eq:varmean},
\[
  \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)_{\text{Laplace}} = \frac{2b^2}{N} = 2 \times \text{CRLB}_{\text{Laplace}}}
\]
This holds for every $N$, not only for large $N$! The mean is still correct on average, but its variance is twice the CRLB, so it needs twice as many samples to be as accurate as the best estimator! The intuitive reason is that every sample gets the same weight $1/N$, including the few large samples that Laplace noise produces, and these pull the mean away from $A$.

\subsection{The sample median}
\label{sec:median}

The noise is symmetric about zero, so the samples are symmetric about $A$, and so is the distribution of the median. So the median is also unbiased. Its exact variance has no simple formula, but for large $N$ the sample median is approximately Gaussian with
variance~\cite[Ch.~21]{vdv}
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{median}) \approx \frac{1}{4N\, p_w(0)^2}.
  \label{eq:varmedian}
\end{equation}
The median only depends on which samples lie above and below $A$, so its accuracy depends on
how many samples fall close to $A$. This is set by the height of the noise density at the centre,
$p_w(0)$. A taller peak puts more samples near $A$ and gives a more accurate median.

For Laplace noise, $p_w(0) = 1/(2b)$, so from \eqref{eq:varmedian}
\[
  \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{median})_{\text{Laplace}} \approx
  \frac{1}{4N \cdot \frac{1}{4b^2}} = \frac{b^2}{N} = \text{CRLB}_{\text{Laplace}}}
\]
So for large $N$ the median reaches the CRLB under Laplace noise. It is asymptotically efficient.

For Gaussian noise, $p_w(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$, so
\[
  \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{median})_{\text{Gauss}} \approx
  \frac{1}{4N \cdot \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}} = \frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{N}
  \approx 1.57 \times \text{CRLB}_{\text{Gauss}}}
\]
Under Gaussian noise, the distance of each sample from $A$ carries information. The median
throws this away, since it only looks at the order of the samples, and this costs about $57\%$
more variance than the mean.

\section{Simulation results}
\label{sec:sims}

In this section we check the results of Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} by simulation, and then look
at skewed noise, where the theory so far does not directly apply. All the code is in the
\texttt{sim/} folder of the project, with one script per simulation.

Every simulation follows the same Monte Carlo method. We fix the true value $A = 1$ and repeat
the experiment $R = 5000$ times. In each trial we draw $N$ fresh noise samples, form
$x[n] = A + w[n]$, and compute both the sample mean and the sample median. This gives $5000$
values of each estimator. Their average estimates $E[\hat A]$, and their sample variance
estimates $\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)$. Dividing the variance by the CRLB gives the measured
efficiency ratio.

Since we only use $5000$ trials, the measured variance is itself a little noisy. For estimates that
are roughly Gaussian, the relative error of a sample variance computed from $R$ values is about
$\sqrt{2/R}$, which is $2\%$ here. So a measured ratio of $0.97$ is in agreement with a predicted
ratio of $1$.

\subsection{Gaussian and Laplace noise}
\label{sec:simA}

We first check the predicted ratios of Table~\ref{tab:predicted}. We use $\sigma = 1$ for the
Gaussian noise and $b = 1$ for the Laplace noise. The ratios do not depend on these values, since
both the variances and the CRLBs scale in the same way. The code is in
\texttt{sim/sim\_A\_mean\_vs\_median.py}.

Table~\ref{tab:simA} shows the measured ratios at $N = 100$ next to the predicted ones. All four
agree with the theory. The mean has ratio $1$ under Gaussian noise and $2$ under Laplace noise,
and the median has ratio close to $\pi/2$ under Gaussian noise.

\subsection{Asymmetric noise}
\label{sec:simB}

So far the noise has been symmetric about zero. In that case the mean and the median both aim at
the same value $A$, and they only differ in their variance. Real noise is often skewed, with a long
tail on one side. For skewed noise the mean and the median of the noise are no longer equal, and
the two estimators aim at different values. In this section we show this, and we predict the
value each estimator reaches before running the simulation.

\paragraph{What each estimator converges to.}
The sample mean converges to the mean of the distribution of $x[n]$, which is
$A + E[w] = A$, since the noise still has zero mean. The sample median balances the samples, with
half on each side, so for large $N$ it converges to the value that splits the distribution of
$x[n]$ in half. This is the median of $x[n]$, which is $A + \operatorname{median}(w)$. So
\[
  \bar x \to A, \qquad \operatorname{median}(\mathbf{x}) \to A + \operatorname{median}(w).
\]
This is the same idea as in Section~\ref{sec:losses}. Squared error leads to the mean and absolute
error leads to the median, and when the noise is skewed these are two different numbers.

\paragraph{Exponential noise.}
Let $E \sim \operatorname{Exp}(1)$, which has mean $1$, and set $w = E - 1$ so that $w$ has zero
mean. The median $m$ of $E$ satisfies
\[
  P(E \leq m) = 1 - e^{-m} = \frac{1}{2},
\]
which gives $m = \ln 2$. So $\operatorname{median}(w) = \ln 2 - 1 \approx -0.307$, and we predict
\[
  \bar x \to 1, \qquad \operatorname{median}(\mathbf{x}) \to 1 + \ln 2 - 1 = \ln 2 \approx 0.693 .
\]

\paragraph{Lognormal noise.}
Let $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Then $e^{Z}$ is lognormal with mean $E[e^Z] = e^{1/2}$, and we set
$w = e^{Z} - e^{1/2}$ so that $w$ has zero mean. Since $e^{z}$ is an increasing function, the median
of $e^{Z}$ is $e$ raised to the median of $Z$, which is $e^0 = 1$. So
$\operatorname{median}(w) = 1 - e^{1/2} \approx -0.649$, and we predict
\[
  \bar x \to 1, \qquad \operatorname{median}(\mathbf{x}) \to 2 - e^{1/2} \approx 0.351 .
\]
The lognormal noise has a much longer right tail than the exponential noise, so it produces a
few very large samples.

Figure~\ref{fig:simB-density} shows both noise densities next to the standard Gaussian. Both have
mean zero, but most of their probability lies to the left of zero, and this is balanced by a long
tail on the right. The shaded area, to the left of the median, holds exactly half of the
probability. The gap between the mean and the median is what the two estimators will see.

\eqref{eq:varmedian}, where for skewed noise $p_w(0)$ is replaced by the density of $w$ at its
median~\cite[Ch.~21]{vdv}. This density is $1/2$ for exponential noise and $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ for

estimate. If $A$ is meant to be the mean of the data, MSE is the right loss. If it is meant to be
the typical value, which the median describes better for skewed data, MAE is the right loss.
Choosing MAE because the data has outliers, as the survey suggests~\cite{terven}, also changes
what is being estimated when the noise is skewed.

\subsection{Summary of the results}

The simulations agree with the theory in both cases. Under symmetric noise both estimators
are unbiased, and the cost of the wrong loss shows up only as extra variance, a factor of $2$ for
the mean under Laplace noise and $\pi/2$ for the median under Gaussian noise. Under skewed noise
the two losses estimate different quantities, the mean and the median of the data.

\section{Heavier tails, the Student-t family}
\label{sec:studentt}

So far we have compared two noise distributions with very different tails, the Gaussian and the
Laplace. In this section we look at a whole family of symmetric noises whose tails can be made
gradually heavier. This shows how the cost of each loss changes as the noise moves away from
Gaussian, and it uses exactly the same tools as Sections~\ref{sec:crlb} and~\ref{sec:mismatch}.

\subsection{The Student-t distribution}

The Student-t distribution with $\nu > 0$ degrees of freedom and scale $s > 0$ has density
\begin{equation}
  p_w(w) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)s}
  \left(1 + \frac{w^2}{\nu s^2}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}} .
  \label{eq:tpdf}
\end{equation}
It is symmetric about zero, so its mean and median are both zero whenever the mean exists. Its
tails fall off like $|w|^{-(\nu+1)}$, which is much slower than the Gaussian $e^{-w^2/2}$. The
parameter $\nu$ controls how heavy the tails are. As $\nu \to \infty$ the density tends to the
Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$, and at $\nu = 1$ it is the Cauchy distribution. Figure~\ref{fig:tdensity}
shows the density for three values of $\nu$ on a log scale, where the difference in the tails is
easy to see.

A useful way to think about the t distribution, given by Lange et al.~\cite{lange}, is as a
Gaussian whose variance is itself random. If $u$ is drawn from a $\chi^2_\nu/\nu$ distribution and
then $w \mid u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2/u)$, the resulting $w$ has the density \eqref{eq:tpdf}. Most
samples come from a Gaussian with variance close to $s^2$, but now and then $u$ is small and the
sample comes from a much wider Gaussian. These occasional wide samples are the heavy tails.
The same paper gives the variance,
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(w) = \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}\,s^2, \qquad \nu > 2,
\]
which is infinite for $\nu \leq 2$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{../figures/simC_t_density.png}
\caption{Student-t densities with $s = 1$ for $\nu = 3$, $5$ and $30$, and the standard Gaussian,
on a log scale.}
\label{fig:tdensity}
\end{figure}

\subsection{CRLB for Student-t noise}

We follow the same steps as in Section~\ref{sec:crlb}. Taking the log of \eqref{eq:tpdf} with
$w = x - A$,
\[
  \ln p_w(x - A) = \text{const} - \frac{\nu + 1}{2}\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{(x-A)^2}{\nu s^2}\right).
\]
Differentiating with respect to $A$ gives the score of one sample,
\begin{align*}
  s(x; A) &= -\frac{\nu+1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + \frac{(x-A)^2}{\nu s^2}}\cdot\left(-\frac{2(x-A)}{\nu s^2}\right) \\
  &= \frac{(\nu + 1)\,w}{\nu s^2 + w^2}.
\end{align*}
Note that the score grows linearly for small $w$, like the Gaussian score, but goes back to zero for
large $|w|$. A sample far out in the tail barely changes the score, which is the first sign that the
matched estimator will ignore outliers.

The Fisher information of one sample is $i(A) = E[s(x;A)^2]$. To compute it we substitute
$t = w/(\sqrt{\nu}\,s)$, so that $w = \sqrt{\nu}\,s\,t$ and $dw = \sqrt{\nu}\,s\,dt$. The score becomes
\[
  s(x; A) = \frac{(\nu+1)\,t}{\sqrt{\nu}\,s\,(1 + t^2)},
\]
and the density becomes $p_w(w)\,dw = c\,(1 + t^2)^{-(\nu+1)/2}\,dt$ with
$c = 1/B\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\nu}{2}\right)$, where $B$ is the Beta function. Then
\begin{align*}
  i(A) &= \frac{(\nu+1)^2}{\nu s^2}\; c \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{t^2}{(1+t^2)^{(\nu+5)/2}}\,dt \\
  &= \frac{(\nu+1)^2}{\nu s^2}\cdot\frac{B\!\left(\tfrac32, \tfrac{\nu+2}{2}\right)}{B\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\nu}{2}\right)},
\end{align*}
using the standard integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} t^2 (1+t^2)^{-p}\,dt = B\!\left(\tfrac32, p - \tfrac32\right)$
with $p = (\nu+5)/2$. Writing the Beta functions in terms of Gamma functions and using
$\Gamma(z+1) = z\,\Gamma(z)$, the ratio simplifies to
\[
  \frac{B\!\left(\tfrac32, \tfrac{\nu+2}{2}\right)}{B\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\nu}{2}\right)}
  = \frac{\Gamma(\tfrac32)}{\Gamma(\tfrac12)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{\nu+2}{2})}{\Gamma(\tfrac{\nu}{2})}
  \cdot\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{\nu+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\tfrac{\nu+5}{2})}
  = \frac12\cdot\frac{\nu}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{(\nu+3)(\nu+1)}
  = \frac{\nu}{(\nu+1)(\nu+3)}.
\]
We thus obtain
\[
  i(A) = \frac{\nu + 1}{(\nu + 3)\,s^2}, \qquad
  \boxed{\text{CRLB}_t = \frac{\nu + 3}{\nu + 1}\cdot\frac{s^2}{N}}
\]
As $\nu \to \infty$ this tends to $s^2/N$, the Gaussian CRLB, as it should.

\subsection{The mean and the median}

The variance of the sample mean follows from \eqref{eq:varmean},
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}\cdot\frac{s^2}{N}, \qquad \nu > 2.
\]
For the median we use \eqref{eq:varmedian} with the height of the density at zero,
\[
  p_w(0) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)s},
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{median}) \approx \frac{1}{4N\,p_w(0)^2}.
\]
Dividing by the CRLB, the efficiency ratios are
\[
  \boxed{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)}{\text{CRLB}_t} = \frac{\nu(\nu+1)}{(\nu-2)(\nu+3)}},
  \qquad
  \boxed{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{median})}{\text{CRLB}_t} \approx \frac{\nu + 1}{4(\nu+3)\,s^2\,p_w(0)^2}}
\]
Both ratios no longer depend on $s$, since $p_w(0)$ is proportional to $1/s$.

These two formulas behave in opposite ways. As $\nu \to \infty$ the mean's ratio goes to $1$ and
the median's goes to $\pi/2$, the Gaussian values of Section~\ref{sec:mismatch}. As $\nu$ decreases
towards $2$, the mean's ratio grows without limit because the variance of the noise blows up,
while the median's ratio stays near $1.2$. At $\nu = 3$, for example, the mean has ratio exactly $2$
and the median has ratio $\pi^2/8 \approx 1.23$. The two curves cross at $\nu \approx 4.68$. For
lighter tails than this the mean is the better estimator, and for heavier tails the median is
better. This crossing point agrees with the value $4.679$ reported in the literature for the
relative efficiency of the median and the mean under t noise.

\subsection{The matched estimator}

The estimator that reaches the CRLB is the MLE for t noise. Setting the total score to zero,
\[
  \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{(\nu + 1)(x[n] - A)}{\nu s^2 + (x[n] - A)^2} = 0 .
\]
If we define the weight of each sample as
\[
  w_n = \frac{\nu + 1}{\nu + r_n^2}, \qquad r_n = \frac{x[n] - A}{s},
\]
then each term of the sum equals $w_n (x[n] - A)/s^2$, and the equation becomes
$\sum_n w_n (x[n] - A) = 0$. Solving for $A$,
\[
  \hat A = \frac{\sum_n w_n\, x[n]}{\sum_n w_n}.
\]
This is a weighted mean, and the weights are exactly those of Lange et al.~\cite[eq.~(3)]{lange}.
The weights depend on $\hat A$ itself, so we compute $\hat A$ by starting from the median and
repeating the weighted mean until it stops changing. A sample far from $A$ has a large $r_n$ and so
a small weight. The t-MLE therefore behaves like the mean for samples near the centre and ignores
samples far in the tails, which is why it can beat both the mean and the median.

\subsection{Infinite variance, $\nu \leq 2$}

For $\nu \leq 2$ the noise has infinite variance, so the sample mean has infinite variance too and
the ratio $\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)/\text{CRLB}$ cannot be used. The CRLB itself still exists, since
the Fisher information $(\nu+1)/((\nu+3)s^2)$ is finite for every $\nu > 0$. To compare the estimators
we use instead the interquartile range (IQR) of the estimates, the width of the middle half of their
distribution, which exists for any distribution.

\paragraph{The median.} From \eqref{eq:varmedian}, the median is still approximately Gaussian, with
standard deviation $1/(2\,p_w(0)\sqrt{N})$. The IQR of a Gaussian is $2 \times 0.6745$ times its
standard deviation, so
\[
  \text{IQR}(\operatorname{median}) \approx \frac{0.6745}{p_w(0)\sqrt{N}} .
\]

\paragraph{The mean at $\nu = 1$.} This is the Cauchy distribution, $p_w(w) = \frac{1}{\pi s}\,\frac{1}{1 + (w/s)^2}$,
which has no mean and no variance. Its characteristic function is $\phi_w(t) = E[e^{jtw}] = e^{-s|t|}$.
The average of $N$ independent noise samples, $\bar w = \frac1N\sum_n w[n]$, therefore has
characteristic function
\[
  \phi_{\bar w}(t) = \left(\phi_w\!\left(\frac{t}{N}\right)\right)^{N}
  = \left(e^{-s|t|/N}\right)^{N} = e^{-s|t|} = \phi_w(t).
\]
So the average of $N$ Cauchy samples has exactly the same distribution as a single sample, and the
sample mean $\bar x = A + \bar w$ is no better than one sample $x[0]$, however large $N$ is. The
standard Cauchy distribution has quartiles at $\pm s$, so the IQR of the mean is $2s$ for every $N$.

\paragraph{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:simC-inf} shows the IQR of both estimators against $N$ for
$\nu = 1$ and $\nu = 2$. For $\nu = 1$ the IQR of the mean stays at $2$ for every $N$, between $1.92$
and $2.09$ in the simulation, exactly as the characteristic function argument predicts, while the IQR
of the median shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$ along its predicted line. For $\nu = 2$ the mean exists and
the IQR of the sample mean does shrink, but more slowly than $1/\sqrt{N}$, because the variance is
still infinite. The median is again on its predicted line.

\section{Skewness, the gamma family}
\label{sec:gamma}

The Student-t family changed the tails of the noise while keeping it symmetric. In this section we
change the skewness instead. The gamma family runs from the exponential noise of
Section~\ref{sec:simB} to noise that is almost Gaussian, so it connects the skewed case to the
symmetric one.

\subsection{The gamma distribution}

A gamma random variable $Y$ with shape $k > 0$ and scale $\theta > 0$ has density
\[
  p_Y(y) = \frac{y^{k-1}\,e^{-y/\theta}}{\Gamma(k)\,\theta^k}, \qquad y > 0,
\]
with mean $k\theta$, variance $k\theta^2$ and skewness $2/\sqrt{k}$. For $k = 1$ it is the
exponential distribution. As $k$ grows the skewness goes to zero and, by the central limit theorem
(a gamma variable with integer $k$ is a sum of $k$ exponentials), the distribution approaches a
Gaussian. To use it as noise we shift it to zero mean,
\[
  w = Y - k\theta, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(w) = k\theta^2 .
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:gammadensity} shows the shape of this noise for several values of $k$, each scaled
to unit variance so that only the shape changes. The noise always has a hard left edge at
$w = -k\theta$ and a long right tail, and both effects fade as $k$ grows.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{../figures/simD_gamma_density.png}
\caption{Gamma noise for $k = 1, 3, 5, 10, 30$, shifted to zero mean and scaled to unit variance,
with the standard Gaussian for comparison.}
\label{fig:gammadensity}
\end{figure}

\subsection{CRLB for gamma noise}

With $x[n] = A + w[n]$, each sample is $x = c + Y$ with left edge $c = A - k\theta$, so $Y = x - c$.
The log-density of one sample is
\[
  \ln p(x; A) = (k-1)\ln(x - c) - \frac{x - c}{\theta} + \text{const}.
\]
Since $c = A - k\theta$, we have $\partial c/\partial A = 1$, and differentiating with respect to $A$ gives
the score
\[
  s(x; A) = -\frac{k-1}{Y} + \frac{1}{\theta}.
\]
To find its moments we need the negative moments of the gamma distribution. From the density,
\[
  E[Y^{-m}] = \frac{\Gamma(k - m)}{\Gamma(k)\,\theta^m}, \qquad k > m,
\]
which gives $E[1/Y] = 1/(\theta(k-1))$ for $k > 1$ and $E[1/Y^2] = 1/(\theta^2(k-1)(k-2))$ for $k > 2$.
First we check the regularity condition. For $k > 1$,
\[
  E[s(x;A)] = -(k-1)\cdot\frac{1}{\theta(k-1)} + \frac{1}{\theta} = 0 .
\]
The Fisher information of one sample is then
\begin{align*}
  i(A) = E[s(x;A)^2] &= (k-1)^2 E\!\left[\frac{1}{Y^2}\right] - \frac{2(k-1)}{\theta}E\!\left[\frac{1}{Y}\right] + \frac{1}{\theta^2} \\
  &= \frac{k-1}{\theta^2(k-2)} - \frac{2}{\theta^2} + \frac{1}{\theta^2} \\
  &= \frac{1}{\theta^2(k-2)}, \qquad k > 2 .
\end{align*}
We thus obtain
\[
  \boxed{\text{CRLB}_\gamma = \frac{(k-2)\,\theta^2}{N}, \qquad k > 2}
\]

Here the support of the data, $x > c$, moves with $A$. Lu et al.~\cite{lu2025} show that the CRLB
still holds in such a case if the expected squared score is finite and the density is zero at the
edge of its support. For $k > 2$ both conditions hold, since the density behaves like $Y^{k-1}$ near
the edge and goes to zero there, so the bound above is valid. For $1 < k \leq 2$ the density still
goes to zero at the edge, but $E[1/Y^2]$ is infinite, so the Fisher information is infinite and the
CRLB gives only the useless bound $0$. For $k \leq 1$ the density does not go to zero at the edge at
all, and the CRLB does not apply. Smith~\cite{smith} studied this non-regular case and showed that estimators based on the
smallest samples can then converge faster than $1/N$. We come back to this in
Section~\ref{sec:pareto} with Pareto noise, where the edge itself moves with $A$.

\subsection{The mean, the matched estimator and the median}

The noise has zero mean, so the sample mean is unbiased, with $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = k\theta^2/N$.
Dividing by the CRLB,
\[
  \boxed{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)}{\text{CRLB}_\gamma} = \frac{k}{k-2}}, \qquad k > 2 .
\]
This is $3$ at $k = 3$ and $1.25$ at $k = 10$, and it goes to $1$ as $k \to \infty$, where the noise
becomes Gaussian.

The estimator that reaches the CRLB is the MLE for gamma noise. Setting the total score to zero,
\[
  \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{1}{x[n] - c} = \frac{N}{\theta(k-1)} .
\]
The left side increases with $c$ and goes to infinity as $c$ approaches the smallest sample, so
this equation has exactly one solution $\hat c$ below $\min_n x[n]$. We find it numerically and set
$\hat A = \hat c + k\theta$.

The median behaves as in Section~\ref{sec:simB}. It converges to $A + \operatorname{median}(w)$, where
$\operatorname{median}(w) = \operatorname{median}(Y) - k\theta$. The gamma median has no closed form, but
it is well approximated by $\theta(k - 1/3)$~\cite{chenrubin}, so
\[
  \operatorname{median}(w) \approx -\frac{\theta}{3} .
\]
This means the median's bias stays close to $-\theta/3$ for every $k$, while the spread of the noise,
$\sqrt{k}\,\theta$, keeps growing. Measured in units of the noise spread, the bias is about
$1/(3\sqrt{k})$, so it fades as the noise becomes less skewed. For $k = 1$ the exact value is
$\ln 2 - 1 \approx -0.307$, the shift we found in Section~\ref{sec:simB}. The variance of the median is
given by \eqref{eq:varmedian} with the density at the median, $1/(4N f(m)^2)$.

\section{A tight bound for the wrong model}
\label{sec:mcrb}

\subsection{Why the CRLB is loose here}

The CRLB of Section~\ref{sec:crlb} is a bound for unbiased estimators of $A$, and it is computed from
the true noise distribution. It tells us the best variance that any unbiased estimator could
achieve if it knew the true noise. An estimator that is built on a wrong noise model, such as the
sample mean under Laplace noise, can never reach this bound, no matter how many samples we use.
Its variance stays a fixed factor above the CRLB. When the estimator is not even aiming at $A$,
like the median under skewed noise, the CRLB does not apply to it at all. So for the estimators
studied in this report the CRLB is a loose bound.

What we need is a bound for estimators that are derived under an assumed model which may differ
from the true one. This is the misspecified Cram\'er--Rao bound (MCRB). It was first derived by
Vuong~\cite{vuong} and later by Richmond and Horowitz~\cite{richmond}, and Fortunati et
al.~\cite{fortunati} give a tutorial review. We follow the notation of~\cite{fortunati}, where $p(x)$
is the true density of one sample and $f(x \mid \theta)$ is the assumed model with parameter $\theta$.

\subsection{The pseudo-true parameter}

If the assumed model is wrong, there may be no value of $\theta$ for which $f(x\mid\theta) = p(x)$. The
natural target is then the value of $\theta$ that makes the assumed model as close as possible to
the truth. Measuring closeness by the Kullback--Leibler divergence~\cite[assumption A1]{fortunati},
\[
  \theta_0 = \arg\min_{\theta} D\big(p \,\|\, f(\cdot \mid \theta)\big)
  = \arg\min_{\theta} \int p(x)\ln\frac{p(x)}{f(x\mid\theta)}\,dx .
\]
The term $\int p \ln p$ does not depend on $\theta$, so this is the same as
\[
  \theta_0 = \arg\max_{\theta} E_p\big[\ln f(x \mid \theta)\big],
\]
where $E_p$ means the expectation under the true density. The value $\theta_0$ is called the
pseudo-true parameter. White~\cite{white} and Huber~\cite{huber67} showed that the MLE computed
under the wrong model converges to $\theta_0$.

We can now find the pseudo-true value for our two estimators.

\paragraph{Gaussian model, the sample mean.} With $f(x\mid A) = \mathcal{N}(A, \sigma^2)$,
\[
  E_p[\ln f(x\mid A)] = \text{const} - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}E_p\big[(x - A)^2\big].
\]
This is largest where $E_p[(x-A)^2]$ is smallest, which is at $A = E_p[x]$. With $x = A + w$ and
$E[w] = 0$, the pseudo-true value is $A_0 = A$. The sample mean aims at the true value for any
zero-mean noise, as we found in Section~\ref{sec:mismatch}.

\paragraph{Laplace model, the sample median.} With $f(x\mid A) = \frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x - A|/b}$,
\[
  E_p[\ln f(x\mid A)] = \text{const} - \frac{1}{b}E_p\big[|x - A|\big].
\]
This is largest where $E_p|x - A|$ is smallest, which is at the median of the true distribution of
$x$, by the same argument as in Section~\ref{sec:losses}. So $A_0 = A + \operatorname{median}(w)$.
This is exactly the shift we predicted and measured in Section~\ref{sec:simB}. The median under
skewed noise is not a broken estimator of $A$. It is an estimator of its own pseudo-true value.

\subsection{The misspecified Cram\'er--Rao bound}

Define, at the pseudo-true value, the two quantities~\cite[eqs.~(2), (3)]{fortunati}
\[
  \mathcal{A} = E_p\!\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(x\mid\theta)\right]_{\theta = \theta_0},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{B} = E_p\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(x\mid\theta)\right)^2\right]_{\theta = \theta_0}.
\]
Both are computed with the assumed model inside and the true density outside. $\mathcal{A}$ is the
average curvature of the assumed log-likelihood, and $\mathcal{B}$ is the variance of the assumed
score. An estimator $\hat\theta$ derived under the assumed model is called MS-unbiased if
$E_p[\hat\theta] = \theta_0$. Theorem 1 of~\cite{fortunati}, due to Vuong~\cite{vuong}, states that
every MS-unbiased estimator from $N$ i.i.d.\ samples satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \geq \text{MCRB} = \frac{1}{N}\,\frac{\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}^2}.
  \label{eq:mcrb}
\end{equation}
For a vector parameter the right side is $\frac1N \mathcal{A}^{-1}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}^{-1}$, which is
why it is often called the sandwich formula.

Two facts make this bound useful. First, if the assumed model is correct, then $\theta_0$ is the
true parameter and $\mathcal{B} = -\mathcal{A}$ is the Fisher information, so \eqref{eq:mcrb} becomes
$1/(N\mathcal{B})$, the ordinary CRLB. The MCRB is a generalisation, not a different theory. Second,
Theorem 2 of~\cite{fortunati} states that the MLE computed under the assumed model is asymptotically
MS-unbiased and its variance tends to the MCRB. So the MCRB is not only a lower bound for the wrong-model
estimator, it is the variance that estimator actually has for large $N$. This is what makes it a
tight bound.

\subsection{Our earlier formulas are MCRBs}

\paragraph{Gaussian model.} For $f(x\mid A) = \mathcal{N}(A, \sigma^2)$,
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial A}\ln f = \frac{x - A}{\sigma^2}, \qquad
  \frac{\partial^2}{\partial A^2}\ln f = -\frac{1}{\sigma^2}.
\]
At $A_0 = A$ we have $x - A_0 = w$, so
\[
  \mathcal{A} = -\frac{1}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mathcal{B} = \frac{E[w^2]}{\sigma^4} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(w)}{\sigma^4},
  \qquad
  \boxed{\text{MCRB}_{\text{mean}} = \frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{\operatorname{Var}(w)/\sigma^4}{1/\sigma^4} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(w)}{N}}
\]
This is \eqref{eq:varmean}. Note that the assumed variance $\sigma^2$ cancels, so a wrong guess of the
noise variance in the Gaussian model has no effect on how well the mean estimates $A$.

\paragraph{Laplace model.} For $f(x\mid A) = \frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x-A|/b}$ the score is
$\operatorname{sign}(x - A)/b$, so $\mathcal{B} = 1/b^2$. The second derivative does not exist at
$x = A$, so we use the form of $\mathcal{A}$ that Huber~\cite{huber67} used for such estimators, the
derivative of the expected score. With $F$ the true distribution function of $x$,
\[
  E_p\!\left[\frac{\operatorname{sign}(x - A)}{b}\right] = \frac{1 - 2F(A)}{b},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{A} = \frac{d}{dA}\,\frac{1 - 2F(A)}{b}\bigg|_{A_0} = -\frac{2 f(m)}{b},
\]
where $f(m)$ is the true density at the median. This agrees with the definition in~\cite{fortunati}
whenever the derivative and the expectation can be swapped. Then
\[
  \boxed{\text{MCRB}_{\text{median}} = \frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{1/b^2}{4f(m)^2/b^2} = \frac{1}{4N f(m)^2}}
\]
which is \eqref{eq:varmedian}, again with the assumed scale $b$ cancelling.

So every variance formula used in Sections~\ref{sec:mismatch} to~\ref{sec:gamma} is an MCRB, and the
efficiency ratios we have been computing are
\[
  \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)}{\text{CRLB}} = \frac{\text{MCRB}}{\text{CRLB}} ,
\]
the price of the wrong model measured against the best possible estimator.

\paragraph{When the pseudo-true value is not $A$.} If $\theta_0$ differs from the true value, the mean
square error about the true value has an extra bias term~\cite[Section~3.4]{fortunati},
\[
  \text{MSE} = E_p\big[(\hat\theta - \theta_{\text{true}})^2\big] \approx \text{MCRB} + (\theta_0 - \theta_{\text{true}})^2 .
\]
For the median under skewed noise the bias is $\operatorname{median}(w)$. The MCRB shrinks like $1/N$
but the bias does not, so for large $N$ the bias dominates.

\subsection{A mistake in the variance}

The MCRB also answers what happens when we estimate the noise variance itself with a wrong model.
We look at two kinds of mistake.

\paragraph{A wrong mean.} This is the example worked out in~\cite[Section~4.2]{fortunati}. The data are
$\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, but the variance is estimated assuming the mean is zero, with the model
$f(x\mid\theta) = \mathcal{N}(0, \theta)$. The MLE under this model is $\hat\theta = \frac1N\sum_n x[n]^2$.
The pseudo-true value is $\theta_0 = E[x^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2$, and the bound is
\[
  \text{MCRB} = \frac{2\sigma^4 + 4\mu^2\sigma^2}{N}, \qquad \text{CRLB} = \frac{2\sigma^4}{N}.
\]
We reproduce this example in simulation as a check of our code.

\paragraph{A wrong noise shape.} Now the noise has zero mean and we again assume the model
$\mathcal{N}(0, \theta)$, but the true noise is not Gaussian. For this model
\[
  \ln f(x\mid\theta) = -\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\theta) - \frac{x^2}{2\theta}, \qquad
  \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f = \frac{x^2 - \theta}{2\theta^2}, \qquad
  \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f = \frac{1}{2\theta^2} - \frac{x^2}{\theta^3}.
\]
Setting the expected score to zero gives the pseudo-true value $\theta_0 = E[x^2] = \sigma^2$, the true
variance, so the estimator aims at the right value. At $\theta_0 = \sigma^2$,
\[
  \mathcal{A} = \frac{1}{2\sigma^4} - \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^6} = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^4}, \qquad
  \mathcal{B} = \frac{E[(x^2 - \sigma^2)^2]}{4\sigma^8} = \frac{\mu_4 - \sigma^4}{4\sigma^8},
\]
where $\mu_4 = E[w^4]$ is the fourth moment of the noise. Then
\[
  \boxed{\text{MCRB}_{\sigma^2} = \frac{\mu_4 - \sigma^4}{N}}
\]
There are now three different numbers one could call a bound.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The CRLB computed from the assumed Gaussian model, $2\sigma^4/N$. This is what one would
        write down if one trusted the Gaussian assumption.
  \item The CRLB computed from the true noise model. This is the best any unbiased estimator of
        $\sigma^2$ could do if it knew the true noise.
  \item The MCRB, the variance that the Gaussian-model estimator actually has.
\end{enumerate}
For Laplace noise with scale $b$ we have $\sigma^2 = 2b^2$ and $\mu_4 = 24b^4$, so
$\text{MCRB} = 20b^4/N$, while the Gaussian-model CRLB is $2(2b^2)^2/N = 8b^4/N$. For the true CRLB we
use the Laplace model with parameter $b$, whose Fisher information per sample is $1/b^2$, and the
transformation rule for the CRLB~\cite{kay}. Since $\sigma^2 = 2b^2$ and $d\sigma^2/db = 4b$,
\[
  \text{CRLB}_{\sigma^2} = \frac{(4b)^2}{N\cdot 1/b^2} = \frac{16 b^4}{N}.
\]
For Student-t noise with $\nu > 4$, using $\mu_4 = 3\nu^2 s^4/((\nu-2)(\nu-4))$ and the Fisher
information of the scale, $2\nu/((\nu+3)s^2)$, the same steps give
\begin{align*}
  \text{MCRB} &= \frac{2\nu^2(\nu-1)}{(\nu-2)^2(\nu-4)}\cdot\frac{s^4}{N}, \\
  \text{CRLB} &= \frac{2\nu(\nu+3)}{(\nu-2)^2}\cdot\frac{s^4}{N}, \\
  \text{Gaussian-model CRLB} &= \frac{2\nu^2}{(\nu-2)^2}\cdot\frac{s^4}{N}.
\end{align*}

The ordering is the important part. For Laplace noise the Gaussian-model CRLB, $8b^4/N$, is below
the true CRLB, $16b^4/N$. So the bound one gets by trusting the wrong model is not a valid bound at
all, since no unbiased estimator can go below $16b^4/N$. The true CRLB is valid but loose for our
estimator, which actually has variance $20b^4/N$, the MCRB. The ratio $\text{MCRB}/\text{CRLB} = 1.25$
is the price of assuming Gaussian noise when estimating the variance of Laplace noise. Dividing the
MCRB by the Gaussian-model CRLB gives $(\mu_4/\sigma^4 - 1)/2$, which depends only on the kurtosis of
the noise. It equals $1$ for Gaussian noise, where the kurtosis is $3$, and $2.5$ for Laplace noise,
where the kurtosis is $6$.

\subsection{Summary}

The CRLB tells us what is possible with the right noise model. The MCRB tells us what we actually
get with the model we assumed, and the estimator built on that model reaches it. Their ratio is the
price of the wrong assumption, which is exactly the efficiency ratio of the earlier sections. The
pseudo-true value explains where a wrong-model estimator ends up, including the median's shift under
skewed noise. For variance estimation, trusting the Gaussian model gives a number that is not even a
valid bound when the noise has heavy tails, while the MCRB gives the correct variance.

\section{When the bound breaks, Pareto noise}
\label{sec:pareto}

Both the CRLB and the MCRB need regularity conditions. In this section we look at a noise for which
these conditions fail, and we see an estimator whose variance falls much faster than any CRLB would
allow. We also give a bound that still holds in this case.

\subsection{The Pareto distribution}

A Pareto random variable $P$ with shape $\alpha > 0$ and minimum value $1$ has density
\[
  p_P(p) = \frac{\alpha}{p^{\alpha + 1}}, \qquad p \geq 1 .
\]
Its tail falls off like a power of $p$, so it is heavy-tailed, and the moment $E[P^r]$ is finite only
for $r < \alpha$. In particular
\[
  E[P] = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha - 1}\ (\alpha > 1), \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(P) = \frac{\alpha}{(\alpha-1)^2(\alpha-2)}\ (\alpha > 2), \qquad
  \operatorname{median}(P) = 2^{1/\alpha}.
\]
Pareto models are used in signal processing for heavy-tailed radar sea clutter~\cite{bocquet}. As
before we shift it to zero mean, $w = P - \alpha/(\alpha - 1)$, which needs $\alpha > 1$.
Figure~\ref{fig:paretodensity} shows the noise for three values of $\alpha$. Unlike every noise so far,
the density does not start at zero. It jumps from $0$ to $\alpha$ at the left edge
$w = 1 - \alpha/(\alpha-1) = -1/(\alpha - 1)$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{../figures/simF_pareto_density.png}
\caption{Pareto noise shifted to zero mean, for $\alpha = 2.5$, $3$ and $5$. The density jumps from
$0$ to $\alpha$ at the left edge.}
\label{fig:paretodensity}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Why the CRLB does not apply}

The data $x[n] = A + w[n]$ live on $x \geq A - 1/(\alpha - 1)$, so the left edge of the support moves
with $A$. Writing $u = x - A + \alpha/(\alpha-1)$ for the value of $P$, the log-density of one sample is
$\ln\alpha - (\alpha + 1)\ln u$, and the score is
\[
  s(x; A) = \frac{\partial}{\partial A}\ln p(x; A) = \frac{\alpha + 1}{u}.
\]
The first regularity condition of the CRLB is that the score has zero mean. Here
\[
  E[s(x;A)] = (\alpha + 1)\,E\!\left[\frac1P\right] = (\alpha+1)\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1} = \alpha \neq 0 ,
\]
so the condition fails. The reason is the edge. When the support depends on $A$, differentiating
$\int p(x;A)\,dx = 1$ with respect to $A$ gives an extra boundary term, equal to the height of the
density at the edge, and this term is only zero when the density is zero there~\cite{lu2025}. For
Pareto noise the density at the edge is $\alpha$.

If we ignore this and compute the CRLB formula anyway, using $E[1/P^2] = \alpha/(\alpha+2)$, we get
\[
  \text{naive CRLB} = \frac{1}{N\,(\alpha+1)^2\,\alpha/(\alpha+2)} = \frac{\alpha + 2}{N\,\alpha(\alpha+1)^2}.
\]
We will see that an estimator beats this number by a large factor, which shows it is not a valid bound.

\subsection{Three estimators}

\paragraph{The mean.} The noise has zero mean, so the sample mean is unbiased, with
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \frac{\alpha}{(\alpha-1)^2(\alpha-2)\,N}, \qquad \alpha > 2 .
\]
For $\alpha \leq 2$ the noise has infinite variance and so does the mean.

\paragraph{The median.} As in Section~\ref{sec:mcrb}, the median goes to its pseudo-true value
$A + \operatorname{median}(w) = A + 2^{1/\alpha} - \alpha/(\alpha-1)$. The density of $P$ at its median is
$\alpha\,2^{-(\alpha+1)/\alpha}$, so from \eqref{eq:varmedian}
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{median}) \approx \frac{2^{2(\alpha+1)/\alpha}}{4N\alpha^2} = \frac{2^{2/\alpha}}{N\alpha^2}.
\]

\paragraph{The minimum.} The edge carries a lot of information. The smallest sample is always just
above the edge, and the edge is at a known distance below $A$. The distribution of the minimum of
$N$ Pareto samples follows from
\[
  P\Big(\min_n P_n > t\Big) = \prod_{n} P(P_n > t) = \left(t^{-\alpha}\right)^N = t^{-N\alpha},
\]
so the minimum is itself Pareto with shape $N\alpha$. Its mean is $N\alpha/(N\alpha - 1)$, so an
unbiased estimator of $A$ is
\[
  \hat A_{\min} = \min_n x[n] + \frac{\alpha}{\alpha - 1} - \frac{N\alpha}{N\alpha - 1},
\]
with variance
\[
  \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\hat A_{\min}) = \frac{N\alpha}{(N\alpha - 1)^2 (N\alpha - 2)} \approx \frac{1}{(N\alpha)^2}}
\]
This falls like $1/N^2$, while the mean, the median and the naive CRLB all fall like $1/N$. So for
large enough $N$ the min-based estimator must go below the naive CRLB. This is possible only
because the CRLB does not apply.

\subsection{A bound that still holds}

The Hammersley--Chapman--Robbins bound~\cite{hammersley, chapmanrobbins} needs no regularity
conditions. For an unbiased estimator of $A$ it states
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(\hat A) \geq \sup_{h \neq 0}\ \frac{h^2}{E_A\!\left[\left(\dfrac{p(\mathbf{x}; A + h)}{p(\mathbf{x}; A)}\right)^{2}\right] - 1},
\]
where the expectation is over the data at the true value $A$. The idea is to compare the true
parameter with a nearby value $A + h$. If the data cannot tell $A$ and $A + h$ apart well, no
estimator can be precise. As $h \to 0$, and when the regularity conditions hold, the bound becomes
the CRLB.

For Pareto noise the ratio is only finite when $h > 0$, because for $h < 0$ the shifted density is
positive where the true one is zero. For $N$ i.i.d.\ samples the expectation is a product,
\[
  E_A\!\left[\left(\frac{p(\mathbf{x}; A+h)}{p(\mathbf{x}; A)}\right)^2\right] = E_1(h)^N, \qquad
  E_1(h) = \int_{1+h}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha\,u^{\alpha+1}}{(u - h)^{2\alpha + 2}}\,du ,
\]
and we find the supremum over $h$ numerically.

smooth, and the CRLB, which assumes a smooth problem, no longer describes what is possible. Neither
squared nor absolute error uses the edge, so both are far from the best estimator here.

\section{Matching the noise in real data}
\label{sec:realdata}

In all the previous sections we chose the noise ourselves. With real data the noise distribution
is unknown, and the first task is to find which family describes it. This is the usual workflow in
applied statistics~\cite{fitdistrplus, gamlss}. We fit several candidate distributions, compare them,
and then use the best description to predict how the mean and the median behave. Here we take the
first steps of this on a real dataset. The gamma family is a natural candidate because its shape
parameter covers everything from very skewed (small $k$) to almost Gaussian (large $k$), as we saw
in Section~\ref{sec:gamma}.

\subsection{The data}

We use the French motor third-party liability claims dataset, \texttt{freMTPL2sev}~\cite{casdatasets},
which contains the amount of each of $26639$ insurance claims. It is a standard benchmark, and the
scikit-learn documentation uses it with a gamma model for claim severity~\cite{sklearntweedie}. For
now we ignore the policy information and treat the claim amounts as samples from one distribution,
so the question is the same as in the DC level problem: what is the typical claim, and how well do
the mean and the median estimate it?

The data are extremely skewed. The mean claim is $2279$ but the median is only $1172$, the standard
deviation is $29297$, and the largest claim is about $4$ million. There is also a feature we never
had in simulation. About $42\%$ of all claims sit exactly on five fixed amounts, the largest group
being $4792$ claims of exactly $1204$. These are most likely standard settlement amounts. No
continuous distribution can describe such spikes, so we expect every fit to be imperfect.

\subsection{Fitting candidate distributions}

We fit five families by maximum likelihood with \texttt{scipy.stats}, fixing the location at
zero for the families that live on positive values. The code for this section is in
\texttt{sim/sim\_G\_real\_data.py}.
We compare them with the Akaike information criterion,
\[
  \text{AIC} = 2p - 2\ln L ,
\]
where $p$ is the number of fitted parameters and $\ln L$ is the maximised log-likelihood, so a lower
AIC means a better fit after paying for extra parameters. We also report the Kolmogorov--Smirnov
statistic, the largest gap between the fitted and the empirical distribution functions.

\midrule

Weibull     & 2 & $-227509$ & $455021$ & 0.204 \\
Gamma       & 2 & $-231154$ & $462312$ & 0.272 \\
Exponential & 1 & $-232593$ & $465188$ & 0.335 \\

\end{table}

The Gaussian is by far the worst fit, which is expected for data that are positive and this
skewed. The lognormal has the lowest AIC and the Weibull the smallest KS statistic, while the gamma
is in between. None of the fits is good, for two reasons that are visible in the figures. The flat
steps in the QQ plots are the spikes of fixed amounts. At the top right, the largest claims bend
sharply above every fitted line, so the real tail is heavier than even the lognormal. A power-law
tail like the Pareto of Section~\ref{sec:pareto} would be a better description of the largest claims.

\subsection{What the gamma shape tells us}

The fitted gamma shape is $k = 0.687$, with scale $\theta = 3315$. Since $k < 1$, the data are more
skewed than the exponential noise of Section~\ref{sec:simB}, with skewness $2/\sqrt{k} = 2.41$ for
the fitted model. From Section~\ref{sec:gamma} this tells us two things. First, the mean and the
median of such data are far apart, so squared error and absolute error estimate very different
values of the typical claim. Indeed the mean is about twice the median. Second, for $k \leq 1$ there
is no usable CRLB for a location parameter, so the efficiency ratios of the earlier sections cannot
even be defined. The choice between the two losses is here a choice of what to estimate, as in
Section~\ref{sec:simB}, not a question of efficiency.

\subsection{The wrong-model standard error}

How precise is the sample mean on these data? There are three ways to answer, and they connect to
Section~\ref{sec:mcrb}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Trust the fitted gamma model. Its variance is $k\theta^2$, so the standard error of the mean
        would be $\sqrt{k}\,\theta/\sqrt{N}$.
  \item Use the sample estimate of the MCRB~\cite[Section~4.1]{fortunati}. For the mean, the sample
        versions of $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ from Section~\ref{sec:mcrb} give the sample variance
        divided by $N$. This uses the data directly and makes no assumption about the noise.
  \item Use the bootstrap. We resample the claims with replacement $2000$ times and take the
        standard deviation of the resampled means.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Estimate & Value & SE from gamma model & SE from sample MCRB & SE from bootstrap \\
\midrule
Mean (MSE)   & 2278.5 & 16.8 & 179.5 & 177.0 \\
Median (MAE) & 1172.0 & --   & --    & 0.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}
\caption{The typical claim estimated by the mean and the median, with standard errors.}
\label{tab:simH-se}
\end{table}

The sample MCRB and the bootstrap agree, at about $178$. The gamma model gives $16.8$, which is
$10.7$ times too small. The fitted gamma has a standard deviation of $\sqrt{k}\,\theta = 2748$, but the
data have $29297$, because the gamma tail is far too light for the largest claims. This is the same
lesson as the variance example of Section~\ref{sec:mcrb}. A precision computed by trusting the
assumed model can be far too optimistic, while the MCRB estimated from the data gives the right
answer.

The median shows the effect of the spikes. The sample median is $1172$, which is itself one of the
fixed amounts with $2072$ claims. Resampling never moves the median off this value, so its bootstrap
standard error is zero. The formula $1/(4Nf(m)^2)$ of Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} assumes a smooth
density at the median, and a spike behaves like an infinitely tall density there, which also gives a
standard error of zero. On these data the median is extremely stable, but only because of how the
claims are recorded.

\subsection{Next steps}

This is a first look at the data, without the policy features. The next steps are to treat the
spikes of fixed amounts separately from the continuous part, as is done with zero-adjusted models in
GAMLSS~\cite{gamlss}, to describe the largest claims with a Pareto tail, and to use the policy
features to compare a gamma regression model with least squares and least absolute deviation
regression. These steps connect this section to the nonlinear models of the project plan.
